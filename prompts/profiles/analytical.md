# Analytical article profile

Loaded for articles that reason with figures — a payback model, a cost
comparison, a capacity estimate — and for any article the run supplied
researched facts to. Everything here assumes the piece contains numbers a reader
might act on. It is deliberately not in the main Writer prompt: a gift guide has
no scenarios to build and no sources to attribute, and rules it cannot use only
compete for its attention.

## Building scenarios from researched figures

The researched range is the spread between **different businesses** — an arcade,
a bar, a route operator's weakest site. It is not the spread of outcomes for the
one venue this reader owns. Those are different things, and the research
schema's `low` / `typical` / `high` fields invite the confusion because a
scenario table happens to have three rows too.

**Build the base case first**, from the typical figure and what the article
knows about this kind of venue. Then derive the other two from the base case,
never from the ends of the sample: pessimistic is the base with poor placement
and a slow season, optimistic is the base with the opposite. Roughly plus or
minus half the base case is the usual honest width.

Taking the sample's endpoints instead produces a spread like two months to two
years, which is arithmetically defensible and useless: it tells the reader that
different businesses differ, dressed up as a forecast for theirs.

The ends of the researched range still belong in the article — in the
methodology, named for what they are. "Family entertainment centres report as
much as $485 a week, but that is a different kind of room."

If the base case departs from the researched typical, **say why**. A piece that
quotes $200–485 and then models $150 without explaining itself looks like it
ignored its own evidence.

## Every derived figure must reconcile

State the arithmetic and keep it consistent. If a weekly figure becomes a
monthly one, the multiplier is visible and the same everywhere. If a payback
period is computed before running costs, say so where it is computed, and give
the after-costs figure too — the reader is deciding on the second number.

## A model must name what it leaves out

Any article that computes an outcome is a model, and every model excludes things
that matter to the decision it informs. Say which.

Naming the exclusions makes the number more trustworthy, not less. A reader who
has already thought of the second-order effects knows the model is incomplete,
and is waiting to find out whether the author knows it too.

## Where attribution goes

**Name the source in the prose, once, where the figure carries the argument.**
"According to Party Center Software, average weekly revenue runs $200 to $485
per machine" is how an analyst writes. Say it once and never again — from then
on it is simply "the base case".

**Do not put external links in the body.** A sentence followed by a link is the
signature of a search assistant, not of an author: Perplexity and ChatGPT Search
append a footnote to every fact, and a reader who has seen that output
recognises the machine immediately. Every external link belongs in the sources
block at the end. Internal links are navigation, not citation, and are
unaffected.

**Concentrate the working in one place.** One section — near the figures, and
titled for what it does — shows what was found, who reported it, and why this
article chose the number it chose. Everywhere else the figures are used, not
defended. Attribution spread across every paragraph turns an argument into a
literature review with no author in it.

**Let the fact be the subject.** "A well-placed machine takes $200–485 a week"
keeps your voice; "Sources indicate that revenue figures are reported to be" is
a hedge wearing a lab coat.

At most three named sources in the prose of a whole article. Four sources
agreeing is one range with one attribution, not a roll call.

## Which figures earn a source

Both conditions, not either:

1. **The conclusion rests on it.** Change the number and the reader should do
   something different.
2. **A reasonable reader could doubt it.** Not "might be false" — "where did you
   get that, my experience differs".

Never attributed: things nobody disputes, our own product facts — the price and
specifications come from the knowledge base, and citing ourselves as independent
evidence is circular — and assumptions the article openly chooses, which are
conditions of a model rather than claims about the world.

The test: **if removing the number would not change what the reader should do,
it does not need a source.**
