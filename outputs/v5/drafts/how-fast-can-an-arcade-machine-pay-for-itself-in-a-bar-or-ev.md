# Arcade Machine for Bar ROI: How Fast Does It Pay Off?

Ask how quickly an arcade machine for a bar pays for itself and you will mostly get two kinds of answer. One is a catalogue page with a price and a photograph and no business case at all. The other is an industry average, quoted with confidence and no working, that turns out on inspection to describe a completely different kind of room.

The honest starting point is an admission. There does not appear to be a published, per-machine revenue study for bars and restaurants. Every weekly earnings figure in circulation comes from arcades and family entertainment centres, where people walked through the door specifically to play. A bar guest came to drink and talk. Same hardware, entirely different attention, and no one seems to have measured the difference.

So rather than borrow someone else's average and hope, this article builds the model in the open. One fixed machine price you can look up. Three clearly labelled scenarios with the arithmetic visible. Every borrowed figure named and its weakness stated. And at the end, a plain accounting of what the model cannot see, because a payback calculation that pretends to capture everything is the least trustworthy kind.

The short version, for anyone who wants it before the detail: a bartop arcade bought outright for a bar plausibly returns its purchase price somewhere between six months and two years. Which end you land on depends far more on your room than on the machine.

## Why payback period is the right question to ask before buying an arcade machine

Return on investment is a flexible number. It can be quoted before costs or after them, over one year or over the life of the equipment, and the person quoting it generally picked the version that flattered the sale. An arcade machine payback period is much harder to bend. It asks a single question - how many months of net income does it take to get the purchase price back - and you can check the answer against your own till at the end of every month.

That matters more here than it does for most equipment a bar buys. A cooler or a card terminal earns its place because the business cannot trade without it, so nobody models it. A machine standing in the corner is discretionary spending, and discretionary spending is exactly where owners get talked into optimism. Payback drags the optimism into daylight, because optimism has to be paid off in weeks.

It also pulls a second question into view: how long the machine actually lasts. A six-month payback on equipment that runs for a decade is a genuinely good purchase. The same six months against something needing replacement in three years is a far thinner proposition. Published estimates of arcade machine service life are inconsistent enough to be worth noticing - one industry publisher gives five to eight years in one article and ten to twenty in another - so the sensible reading is that lifespan depends heavily on how the machine is treated, and that your payback window should be short enough that lifespan never becomes the deciding variable.

The rest of this piece shows its working for the answer given above. Six months to two years is a wide bracket, and the width is not evasion. It is the actual finding.

## The cost baseline: anchoring the analysis to a real machine price

Every payback calculation is only as good as the number at the top of it, and this is where most arcade ROI writing quietly goes vague. Published price guides put a standard commercial video or skill cabinet at roughly $4,000 to $10,000, with licensed and redemption machines running well beyond that. A range that wide is not an input to anything. To calculate a payback period you need one figure a reader can look up and disagree with.

This article uses **$4,250**, the list price of the JVL ECHO HD3 Commercial Edition. Two reasons for that choice. It is a real, published, edition-specific price rather than a blended average, and it sits unremarkably inside the published range for commercial-grade equipment, so nothing in the model is quietly resting on an unusually cheap or unusually expensive machine.

What the price includes matters, because a machine in a venue has to be able to take money. The Commercial Edition ships with a bill acceptor handling $1, $5, $10 and $20 notes with a 500-bill capacity, and a coin acceptor for quarters. That payment hardware is the real dividing line between a commercial arcade machine and a home one, and it is the clearest difference between the two editions: the Home Edition is a free-play machine with neither acceptor, and no revenue model attached to it at all. The two are kept strictly apart for the rest of this article.

Four things sit deliberately outside the anchor. Freight to your venue, anything your own location requires of you locally, the cost of the counter or floor space the machine occupies, and repair. The first two vary too much between venues to model responsibly. The third is real, and it gets its own treatment later. The fourth is left out because it varies with use and with warranty status: the $40 a month used below covers routine upkeep only and does not attempt to model repair cost. Both editions carry a one-year all-inclusive manufacturer warranty on the same terms - confirm current terms with JVL directly.

## How this model is built: methodology and assumptions behind the three scenarios

Before any numbers, the weakness in all of them. According to Party Center Software, average arcade game revenues run between $200 and $485 per game per week. Several other industry sources land in the same territory, with roughly $200 a week treated as a normal expectation for a reasonably trafficked machine. Those figures are consistent, they are repeated widely, and every one of them describes arcades and family entertainment centres.

That is a different building with a different customer. In an arcade, the machine is the reason the guest is standing there. In a bar, the machine is competing with a conversation, a television, and the second half of somebody's drink. Nobody appears to have isolated a bar or restaurant in a per-machine revenue study, so the choice is between pretending the arcade figure transfers cleanly and adjusting it on stated judgement.

This model adjusts. It applies a flat 15% discount to the published typical and high figures, and calls that discount what it is: a judgement about attention, not a measurement. The published typical of $200 becomes $170 a week. The published high of $485 becomes about $410. If your view of bar traffic is harsher or kinder than mine, change the percentage and rerun it - it is two divisions, and the whole point of showing the working is that you can.

The low end is left alone. The lowest figures in circulation already come from thin-traffic contexts, and one operator-facing source reports an average nearer $20 a week across an entire mixed route with its dead locations included. That is not a floor for a well-placed machine, but it is a useful reminder that any published average has silent machines inside it.

One sanity check makes all of this concrete. Published pay-per-play pricing generally sits between $0.50 and $1.00. At a dollar a play, $170 a week is 170 plays, which is about 24 plays a day. Before you accept any benchmark in this article, ask whether 24 plays a day is plausible in your room on a normal week. That question will tell you more than the benchmark will.

Finally, the fixed assumptions. The venue buys the machine outright, so there is no revenue split with a route operator. A month is 4.33 weeks. Running costs are subtracted from month one. The cost of capital and staff time are excluded from the payback figure and picked up again later.

## Three scenarios: pessimistic, base case, and optimistic weekly revenue

With the price fixed and the method stated, the three cases below differ in exactly one input. Weekly revenue moves; the $4,250 machine, the $40 a month of running costs and the 4.33-week month all stay where they are. Whatever spread you see in the payback column is therefore purely the spread in how much a room actually plays, which is the honest way to present a variable nobody has measured for bars.

Read them as brackets rather than forecasts. The pessimistic case is not a disaster; it is what an ordinary, unremarkable placement looks like when nothing goes wrong and nothing goes especially right either. The optimistic case is not a fantasy; it is the published high end with the bar discount applied. Both remain modelled assumptions built on arcade-context source data, not measured bar outcomes, and neither should be read as a promise about your venue.

| Scenario | Weekly revenue | Monthly revenue | Net of running costs | Payback on $4,250 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pessimistic | $50 | $217 | $177 | ~24 months |
| Base case | $170 | $736 | $696 | ~6 months |
| Optimistic | $410 | $1,775 | $1,735 | ~2.5 months |

The distance between the ends of that table is close to ten to one, and that gap is itself the finding. Anyone who answers "how much does an arcade machine make" with a single confident number is either guessing or selling. What follows is each row, with the assumptions that produce it spelled out so you can decide which one your room resembles.

### Pessimistic scenario: weekly revenue, assumptions, and payback period

The pessimistic case assumes $50 a week, which at a dollar a play is around 50 plays, or seven a day. Picture a steady neighbourhood bar with respectable but unspectacular trade, the machine placed somewhere out of the main sightline, no signage, no staff drawing attention to it, and a customer base that skews to quiet weeknights. Nothing is broken in this scenario. The machine simply sits in a room that has not been organised around it.

At $50 a week, monthly revenue is about $217. Subtract $40 for electricity and routine upkeep and you are left with roughly $177 a month, which returns $4,250 in about 24 months. Two years is a long time to wait on a discretionary asset, but it is not automatically a bad outcome - the machine is still ahead of its purchase price well inside most estimates of its service life.

The scenario gets uncomfortable when running costs move to the top of the published range. A machine left powered twelve hours a day with heavier upkeep could carry closer to $95 a month, which cuts the net to about $122 and stretches payback past 34 months. Nearly three years is the point at which lifespan stops being a footnote and starts being the deciding variable, and the disciplined reading is that if this is the realistic case for your room, the machine should either not be bought outright or not be bought at all. That is a genuine possible outcome of this analysis, and it would be dishonest to present it as merely the slow lane.

### Base case (most likely) scenario: the $170/week figure and why the article assumes it

The base case assumes $170 a week, which is the widely reported $200 typical with the 15% bar discount applied. In play terms that is roughly 24 plays a day at a dollar. The room it describes has decent weekend trade, the machine sits where people can see it from the bar, a handful of regulars use it without being prompted, and on a busy Friday two people are occasionally waiting for a turn. It is an ordinary good placement, not a showpiece.

At $170 a week the machine takes about $736 a month. After $40 of running costs, net income is roughly $696, and $4,250 divided by $696 is 6.1 months. Call it six months, and budget seven if you want slack for a slow first few weeks while the room notices the machine exists.

Six months looks fast against the payback benchmarks you will find quoted for amusement equipment generally, which cluster somewhere between six months and two years. The difference is worth understanding rather than celebrating: those benchmarks are usually sizing a whole arcade floor or a major attraction inside one, and they are carrying rent, staffing and fit-out. A single machine dropped into a bar that is already open, already staffed and already paying rent carries none of that overhead. Its payback is faster for a structural reason, not because it is a better business.

This is the figure worth planning around. It is deliberately below the published typical, it is checkable against a plays-per-day question any owner can answer honestly, and a six-month payback on equipment with a service life measured in years is a sound purchase by any ordinary standard of arcade profitability.

### Optimistic scenario: weekly revenue, assumptions, and payback period

The optimistic case assumes $410 a week, the published high end of $485 with the same 15% discount applied. That is roughly 410 plays a week, close to 60 a day. A great deal has to be true at once for that to happen. The room needs genuine weekend volume, the machine needs prominent placement where waiting customers meet it, staff need to mention it without being asked, and the machine needs to have become part of how groups spend an evening rather than something one person tries alone. Leaderboards and head-to-head play matter here, because repeat play is what separates 60 plays a day from six.

The arithmetic is straightforward. At $410 a week the machine takes about $1,775 a month; after $40 of running costs, $1,735; against $4,250 that is a payback of roughly two and a half months. On paper it is an excellent arcade rate of return.

The warning attached to it is more important than the number. This figure descends from venues where playing was the reason the customer came through the door, and even after the discount it still carries that origin. It is achievable in a bar that has deliberately built its evenings around the machine, and there are rooms like that. It is not a number to sign a purchase order against, and if a supplier quotes you something in this territory as an expectation rather than a ceiling, they are selling rather than modelling. Treat the optimistic case as the shape of the upside, not as the plan.

## How probable is each scenario, and which one should an operator actually plan around

There is no honest way to attach probabilities to those three rows. Doing so would require a distribution of real bar placements, and that data does not appear to exist publicly - inventing percentages here would undo the point of the whole exercise. What can be done instead is to name the variables that determine which bracket you land in, because unlike a probability, those are things you control.

- **Sightline.** Whether a guest standing at the bar can see the machine without going looking for it.
- **Hours and crowd.** A room that fills on two nights a week is modelling two nights of play, not seven.
- **Price per play.** At $0.50 you need twice the plays for the same revenue; at $1.00 you may lose the casual single try.
- **Staff behaviour.** A bartender who points at it during a wait is worth more than any amount of signage.
- **Existing games culture.** Rooms that already run darts, pool or a quiz night have customers who expect to play something.

The sensible approach is to split the difference deliberately. Plan your capital around the pessimistic case, so that a 24-month payback would not put pressure on the business, and plan your expectations around the base case, so that six or seven months is what you are actually working toward. Treat the optimistic case as upside you earn through placement and attention rather than as a return you purchased.

There is also a decision rule hiding in that. If a two-year payback genuinely would be a problem for your accounts, the answer is not to buy optimistically - it is to not buy outright. Placing a machine through a route operator on a revenue share moves the capital risk off your books in exchange for a share of the take, with reported splits commonly running somewhere between 50/50 and 70/30. Under that model none of the arithmetic above applies, because there is no purchase price to recover. It earns less per play and costs nothing to be wrong about.

And of the five variables listed, placement is the one that is free to fix. Moving a machine costs an afternoon. A machine in a corridor is a machine in the pessimistic bracket, and it will stay there until somebody moves it.

## After payback: net monthly profit by scenario, with recurring costs named and subtracted

Payback is the start of the interesting part, not the end of it. Once the purchase price is recovered, the machine stops being a project and becomes a line on the monthly P&L, and that line is what determines whether it was worth the counter space over five or ten years.

The recurring costs used throughout this model are modest and deserve naming. BusinessDojo puts machine electricity at roughly $20 to $70 a month for a unit running ten to twelve hours daily, and routine maintenance at $100 to $300 a year, which works out around $8 to $25 a month. Combined, that gives a range of roughly $28 to $95. This model uses $40 as a working figure. It is a derived estimate assembled from two separate line items in general arcade cost data, not a bar-specific measurement, and it should be treated as an assumption you can replace with your own metered numbers.

| Scenario | Monthly revenue | Running costs | Net monthly | Net annual |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pessimistic | $217 | $40 | $177 | ~$2,124 |
| Base case | $736 | $40 | $696 | ~$8,352 |
| Optimistic | $1,775 | $40 | $1,735 | ~$20,820 |

Two costs are missing from that table because no source quantified them and I will not invent figures. The first is staff time: somebody empties the drop, counts it and reconciles it, and in a small bar that person is usually the owner. The second is replacement. After payback there is no depreciation in cash terms, but the machine is still ageing, and setting aside even $35 a month toward the next one turns the pessimistic row into something close to break-even.

What the table means in practice is worth saying plainly. Around $2,100 a year is real money but it sits inside the noise of a bar's accounts; you would not restructure anything around it. Roughly $8,350 a year is a line worth managing actively - worth moving the machine for, worth mentioning to staff, worth thinking about a second unit. The optimistic row describes a venue where the machine has stopped being equipment and become part of the offer, and those venues generally know it already.

## What this model does not count: goodwill, repeat visits, and spend the machine influences but the coin drop never shows

Every model excludes things that matter, and this one excludes a category that bar owners talk about constantly. The coin drop records a play. It does not record the guest who ordered another round while waiting for a turn, the group of four who stayed forty minutes longer than they meant to, the pair who came back on Friday because there was something to do besides talk, or the review that mentions the machine by way of describing the room. Those effects are real and most operators believe in them.

They are also unmeasured. Sources describe qualitatively that games extend dwell time and support drink sales in games-led venues, but nothing found in this research quantified a dollar or percentage lift attributable to a single machine. So no figure appears here for it. Putting an invented number on goodwill would make the model look more complete and make it considerably less trustworthy, which is a bad trade.

The uncounted column has entries on the other side too, and they are mentioned less often. Counter or floor space has an opportunity cost, and in a small room that cost is measured in covers. A machine positioned badly interferes with service. Sound competes with conversation in a room that trades on conversation. And a machine that visibly nobody plays is worse than no machine, because it reads as a room that tried something and gave up.

The practical way to use all this is as a tiebreaker rather than a justification. If the coin drop alone gets you to a payback period you can live with, the uncounted upside is a reason to feel good about a decision you have already justified. If it does not, goodwill will not rescue it, partly because you cannot manage what you cannot measure, and partly because the same guests who might stay for another round are equally capable of walking straight past.

What carries across from the analysis above is not the money - it is the standard. A machine specified to survive a commercial room is specified well past what a basement asks of it, and that is a reasonable answer to the quiet worry that sits behind a purchase like this at home: whether the thing will still feel substantial once the excitement wears off.

## FAQ

### Does the type of bar affect how profitable an arcade machine is?

Yes — bar type shapes both traffic and dwell time, the two biggest levers in any revenue model. A sports bar or pub with regulars who linger performs differently than a fast-turnover cocktail bar where guests rarely stay long enough to play. Venues that already run darts, pool, or trivia nights tend to see stronger arcade usage, because customers already expect an activity, not just a drink.

### Should a bar buy a used arcade machine to shorten the payback period?

A used machine can lower the upfront cost, but it usually comes with an unknown maintenance history and no manufacturer warranty, which shifts risk rather than removing it. A single repair bill mid-lifespan can erase months of the savings a lower purchase price appeared to offer. Buying new with a full warranty trades a slightly higher commercial arcade machine cost for a payback period you can actually plan around.

### How much floor or counter space does a bar need to fit an arcade machine?

Bartop-style cabinets are built to sit on an existing counter or a small stand rather than require a dedicated floor footprint, which is what makes a bartop arcade for bar use practical in rooms with limited square footage. The real space question is less about the machine's size and more about clearance for a player to stand without blocking a walkway or service area. A corner, the end of the bar, or a wall away from the main service flow usually works better than the centre of the room.

### Does a bar need a special license to operate a coin-operated arcade machine?

Rules vary by city, county, and state, and can cover anything from amusement device permits to how coin income is taxed, so no single article can answer this for every location. It is worth checking with local licensing authorities before installing a coin- or bill-operated machine commercially. This question does not apply to a home arcade machine with no payment hardware, since there is no cash being collected.

### Can an arcade machine take up space that would otherwise seat paying customers?

In a small room, yes — a machine placed where a stool or a two-top would otherwise go has a real opportunity cost that a coin-drop figure never captures. The trade-off tends to work in the machine's favour near a bar counter, doorway, or waiting area where seating wasn't practical anyway. It works against the machine if it displaces even one or two covers a night in a room already running near capacity.

### Is a bartop arcade machine a good fit for a restaurant rather than a bar?

The economics depend on dwell time rather than the label on the venue — a restaurant with a bar area or a regular waiting crowd can perform similarly to a bar with comparable foot traffic. A fast-turnover dining room with little standing space or wait time gives guests less opportunity to play, which pulls results toward the pessimistic end of any revenue range. The placement variables that matter — visibility, staff mention, existing games culture — apply the same way regardless of whether the venue serves food or drinks.

### What happens to bar revenue from an arcade machine if it needs repair?

Every day the machine is down is a day of lost coin-drop revenue, which is why service turnaround matters as much as the repair itself for any income projection. Neither this article's model nor most public industry sources quantify typical downtime for a bar machine, so it isn't built into the payback figures above. A manufacturer warranty typically covers the machine and the underlying issue, but who pays for transit and how long that step takes are separate questions worth confirming with the manufacturer before buying.
