## Why Arcade Machines Are Making a Comeback in Bars and Event Venues

Bars have a simple problem. Guests can drink at home for a fraction of the price. What they cannot do at home is share a room with friends and a bit of easy competition.

That is why so many venues have added darts boards, shuffleboard, trivia nights and pool tables over the past decade. None of these are new ideas. They work because they give people a reason to stay one more round. Anything that gives a group a reason to linger tends to justify itself. It does that before it ever shows up as a revenue line.

Arcade formats sit in that same family. They are quick to learn. They suit two people as easily as one. And for guests in their fifties and sixties, they carry a pull that a TV screen never will. Many of them first played these games in a bar, not a bedroom.

There is also a practical reason for the return. A bartop unit takes up a corner of counter, not a wall. Floor space in a bar earns money. Counter space at the end of the bar often does not. A compact machine turns dead space into something guests use.

It also helps to know the format has real production history behind it. JVL has built bartop machines for more than 30 years. JVL bartops have been awarded "Best Bartop Video Game of the Year" by RePlay Magazine multiple times. That is a track record across the bartop line, not a claim about any single model. But it tells you the format is proven, not a passing trend.

So the question is not whether guests will play. The question is whether the play adds up to enough money, fast enough, to justify the price. That is what the rest of this article works out.

## How Arcade Machine ROI Actually Works
![Close-up of a coin acceptor and bill validator slot on a bartop arcade machine, showing the precision mechanical components.](https://zvsboppttbftxdsbcxym.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/article-images/how-fast-can-an-arcade-machine-pay-for-itself-in-a-bar-or-ev/inline-01.png)

Payback math on a machine is not complicated. It has five moving parts, and each one can swing your result by a wide margin.

1. **How many plays per week.** Driven by foot traffic and where the machine sits.
2. **Average spend per play.** Usually a dollar or two, sometimes more on head-to-head play.
3. **Your share of the money.** If the venue is not yours, you split the take.
4. **Recurring costs.** Repairs, cleaning, power, the occasional service call.
5. **Slow weeks.** Quiet months pull the yearly average down.

The trap is that almost everyone guesses high on the first variable. They forget the fourth and fifth completely.

Here is the shape of the calculation, using the $4,250 USD list price of the JVL ECHO HD3 Commercial Edition as the fixed anchor:

**Weekly plays × spend per play = gross weekly take.**  
**Gross take × your share − weekly costs = your weekly net.**  
**$4,250 ÷ your weekly net = weeks to payback.**

That is the whole model. Every number in it except the price is a guess, and a guess you can improve with a month of real data.

The honest way to handle this is not to pick one number. It is to run the math three times, at three levels of hope, and see how wide the gap is. If even the low case pays back inside a couple of years, the risk is small. If only the high case works, you are betting on luck.

### Foot Traffic and Venue Type

Foot traffic drives everything else. A machine in a busy neighbourhood bar on a Friday night sees dozens of guests walk past it. The same machine in a quiet lounge might sit untouched for whole evenings.

But raw head count is only half the story. What matters more is whether guests have time on their hands. A place where people wait - for a table, for a lane, for a round of food - turns waiting into play. A place where everyone is seated and served fast does not.

That is why placement matters as much as traffic. A machine tucked behind a service door earns nothing, no matter how full the room is. Put it at the end of the bar, or beside the spot where people stand and talk, and the same room produces a very different number.

A rough way to sort venues by traffic quality:

- **Strong:** guests linger, groups form, there is standing room near the bar
- **Mixed:** busy but fast turnover, most guests seated and quickly served
- **Weak:** low evening traffic, no natural waiting area, guests arrive and leave in pairs

One more caution. Weekend traffic is not the week. Many owners count a Saturday night, multiply by seven, and get a number that never happens. Count the slow Tuesday too. Your yearly average lives much closer to Tuesday than to Saturday.

### Revenue Split Between Venue and Machine Owner

If you own both the venue and the machine, you keep all of it. That is the simple case, and it gives the fastest payback of any setup.

More often, two parties are involved. An operator owns the machine and places it in someone else's bar. The take gets split. A 50/50 share is the arrangement most often described, and it is a reasonable planning assumption. Treat it as an estimate, not a rule - terms vary by market, by machine, and by who is asking.

The split changes your payback more than almost anything else. Cut your share in half and you double the number of weeks to break even. That is worth thinking through before you agree to anything.

| Who owns what | Typical share to machine owner | Effect on payback |
|---|---|---|
| You own venue and machine | 100% | Fastest |
| Operator places machine in a bar | Around 50% | Roughly double the weeks |
| Venue takes a larger cut | Below 50% | Slower again |

If you own a home bar and rent it out now and then, you are in the first row. You keep the whole take. But your traffic is far lower than a public bar, so the two effects push against each other.

There is one other term to settle early: who pays for repairs. In many operator deals, the machine owner covers service. If you are the venue and someone else's machine sits on your counter, make sure that is written down.

### Seasonality, Maintenance, and Electricity Costs

These three costs are where most online ROI math quietly falls apart. They get skipped, and the payback looks faster than it is.

**Slow seasons.** Most venues have them. A patio bar dies in winter. A lake-house rental dies outside summer. A city pub near offices dies in August. Plan on a few weeks a year at half your normal take, or lower. The simple fix is to build your model on 44 to 48 earning weeks, not 52.

**Repairs and cleaning.** Screens get sticky. Power cords get pulled. Someone spills a drink. As an illustrative estimate, budget $15 to $40 a month for cleaning supplies, small parts, and the odd service visit. Machines with fewer moving parts cost less to keep running. Touch controls have no buttons or joysticks to wear out, which removes a common failure point. A reinforced case and a precision-built frame matter here too, because bar life is hard on furniture.

Coverage is a one-year all-inclusive manufacturer warranty, with return shipping covered both ways. That means year-one repair risk sits with the maker, not you. It is worth knowing what year two looks like before you buy.

**Power.** This one worries people more than it should. Assume 150 watts while switched on and running twelve hours a day. At $0.15 per kWh, that is roughly $8 a month. Even if your real draw is double that, power is a rounding error against a $4,250 machine. Both the wattage and the rate are estimates - check your own bill and your own machine's rating.

Add it up and the recurring cost is small. Call it $25 to $50 a month in a normal year. It will not sink the case. But leave it out and your payback estimate is wrong from the start.

## Three Honest Scenarios: Pessimistic, Neutral, and Optimistic

Now the numbers. Three runs of the same math, three sets of assumptions.

Every figure below is an illustrative estimate. None of it is sourced industry data. The only hard number is the $4,250 USD list price of the JVL ECHO HD3 Commercial Edition, which we use as the fixed cost to recover.

To keep the three cases comparable, all of them share these ground rules:

- Machine cost to recover: **$4,250 USD**
- Earning weeks per year: **46** (allowing for slow weeks)
- Recurring costs: **repairs, cleaning, and power**, named and subtracted
- Revenue split: **stated separately in each case**

And one thing to keep in mind while reading. These three are not equally likely. The neutral case is the one to plan around. If the neutral case does not work for you, the answer is probably no - regardless of how good the optimistic case looks.

We have kept the arithmetic simple on purpose. Round numbers, no hidden steps. If you disagree with an assumption, change it and run it again.

### Pessimistic Scenario: Assumptions and Weekly Revenue

This is the version where things go quietly wrong. Not a disaster - just a slow room, a poor spot, and a split that is not in your favour.

**Assumptions (all illustrative estimates):**

- 40 plays per week
- $1.25 average spend per play
- Gross weekly take: **$50**
- Revenue split: 50% to the machine owner
- Your weekly share: **$25**
- Recurring costs: about $10 a week
- **Weekly net: roughly $15**

At $15 a week, $4,250 takes about 283 earning weeks to recover. At 46 earning weeks a year, that is roughly six years.

Six years is a long time. In plain terms, this case does not work as a money decision. If your honest read of your own room looks like this, do not buy the machine to make money.

But that is not the only reason to buy one. If you also value the machine as something guests enjoy, or as something you enjoy yourself, that changes the picture. A slow payback is not the whole story. Plenty of things in a bar - the good sound system, the comfortable seats - never pay back on their own either.

How likely is this case? Real, but not the norm in a working bar with decent traffic. Call it roughly a one-in-five outcome. It becomes much more likely in a low-traffic room, or with poor placement, or if nobody ever mentions the machine to guests.

### Neutral (Base Case) Scenario: Assumptions and Weekly Revenue

This is the case to plan around. A steady bar, a sensible spot at the end of the counter, and no special effort beyond keeping the screen clean.

**Assumptions (all illustrative estimates):**

- 90 plays per week
- $1.50 average spend per play
- Gross weekly take: **$135**
- Revenue split: 50% to the machine owner
- Your weekly share: **$67.50**
- Recurring costs: about $10 a week
- **Weekly net: roughly $57.50**

At $57.50 a week, $4,250 takes about 74 earning weeks. At 46 earning weeks a year, that is a payback period of roughly 19 months.

A year and a half to recover the full price is a fair result for a fixed asset in a bar. It is slower than the numbers you will see on other sites. It is also more likely to be what you actually get.

Now run the same room where you own both the bar and the machine. You keep the whole $135. Take off $10 in weekly costs and your net is $125 a week. Payback drops to about 34 weeks - under nine months. That single change matters more than any other lever in this article.

How likely is this case? This is the middle of the range for a venue with reasonable evening traffic and a machine people can see. Roughly a one-in-two outcome, which is why it is the planning number.

### Optimistic Scenario: Assumptions and Weekly Revenue

This is the good version. A busy room, a spot people walk right past, staff who point it out, and a small league or tournament night that gives regulars a reason to come back.

**Assumptions (all illustrative estimates):**

- 180 plays per week
- $1.75 average spend per play
- Gross weekly take: **$315**
- Revenue split: 50% to the machine owner
- Your weekly share: **$157.50**
- Recurring costs: about $12 a week
- **Weekly net: roughly $145**

At $145 a week, $4,250 takes about 30 earning weeks - a payback period of well under a year. If you own the venue and keep the full take, it drops to around 14 weeks.

Those are strong numbers, and they are real coin-op arcade profit when the conditions line up. But look at what has to be true. Traffic has to be high. Placement has to be right. Someone has to promote it. And the room has to hold that pace across most of the year, not just in December.

How likely is this case? Less likely than either of the other two. Roughly one in five, and it usually follows deliberate effort rather than luck. Treat it as upside, not as a plan. If you buy the machine expecting this outcome and get the neutral one, you will feel let down by a perfectly reasonable result.

## Which Venue Types Tend to See the Strongest Arcade Returns

Not every room suits a machine. The pattern is fairly consistent, and it comes down to one question: do guests have spare time while they are there?

**Rooms that tend to do well:**

- Neighbourhood bars with regulars and standing room near the counter
- Bars where guests wait - for tables, food, or a pool table
- Sports bars between games, when the screens go quiet
- Bowling alleys, pool halls, and similar spots where waiting is built in
- Private clubs and lounges with steady evening use

**Rooms that tend to struggle:**

- Fine dining, where guests stay seated all evening
- Fast-turnover spots where nobody has ten spare minutes
- Rooms with no free counter or corner in a visible spot
- Seasonal venues open only a few months a year

Event venues are a special case. A rented hall or a wedding space has huge crowds on some nights and nothing on others. Payback there depends on how often the room is booked, and whether the machine is set to free play as an included extra rather than a paid one. If it is free to guests, it is not earning coins - it is helping the room get booked. That is still worth something, but it does not show up in this math.

A home bar sits at the far end of the range. Traffic is low, so coin income will be low too. But you keep everything, there is no split to negotiate, and the reason for owning it was never really the money.

## Practical Ways to Protect or Improve Your Payback Timeline

You have more control here than the scenarios suggest. Most of the gap between the neutral and optimistic cases comes from choices, not luck.

1. **Move it before you judge it.** Try two or three spots over a month each. A machine people walk past earns far more than one they have to look for. This is the cheapest change you can make.
2. **Keep the screen clean.** A sticky or smeared screen quietly kills play. Wipe it nightly. It costs nothing and protects your whole model.
3. **Have staff mention it.** One line from a bartender to a waiting pair - "there's a game at the end of the bar" - moves the numbers more than any sign.
4. **Run a weekly night.** Leaderboards and small tournaments give regulars a reason to return on a set evening. That turns one-off plays into a habit.
5. **Track a real month.** Write down the weekly take for four weeks. Then redo the math in this article with your own figures instead of ours. Everything after that is an informed decision.
6. **Fix faults fast.** A machine out of service earns zero. Know who to call and how long parts take before you need to.

One thing worth checking before you buy: does the machine need Wi-Fi or software updates to run? A unit that works fully offline has fewer ways to fail on a busy Friday. No network means no dropped connection, no update stuck halfway, and nothing to configure when the room is full.

The same goes for what is already loaded on it. A machine with a broad built-in library covers more guests - card games for one group, puzzles for another, bar-sports and trivia for a table. Variety keeps people coming back, and repeat play is the whole engine of payback.

## What You Now Know, and What to Do Next

You now have a model you can check rather than a number you have to trust.

The short version: a $4,250 machine in a typical bar, on a 50/50 split, plausibly pays for itself in around 19 months. Own the room and keep the whole take, and that drops to roughly nine months. After payback, the same machine plausibly nets about $250 a month on the base case - closer to $540 if you own the venue. Every one of those figures is an illustrative estimate, and the neutral case is the one to plan around.

Here is the sensible next step, in order:

1. Judge your own room honestly against the venue-fit list above.

## FAQ

### Is an arcade machine a better bar investment than a pool table or jukebox?

Pool tables have low per-play revenue but almost no upkeep, while jukeboxes have lost pull now that guests carry their own music library in their pocket. An arcade machine sits in between: it's screen-based, easy to track per play, and works for one or two guests at once. There's no universal winner here — the right choice depends on your room's foot traffic and whether guests already gather near a spot where a machine could sit.

### Is it better to rent, lease, or buy an arcade machine for a venue?

Buying outright, which is what the payback math in this article assumes, means the machine becomes a fixed asset you fully own once it earns back its cost. Leasing spreads the outlay but usually costs more over time and rarely hands you the full earnings once the term ends. An operator-placement deal — where someone else buys the machine and splits the take with the venue — lets a bar test the format without paying for hardware at all.

### Does adding a second arcade machine to a venue double the revenue?

Rarely. Revenue from one machine is capped by how many guests have spare time to play, and a second unit usually splits that same pool of players rather than doubling it. A second machine tends to help only when the first one is regularly busy and guests are visibly waiting for a turn — otherwise it just adds a second fixed cost against the same traffic.

### Can a used or refurbished arcade machine make sense for a bar or venue?

A lower upfront price could shorten the payback timeline shown in this article's math, but bars put far heavier daily wear on a machine than a home room does, and used units typically arrive without a manufacturer warranty covering that use. Any saving on price has to be weighed against the risk of downtime with no support line to call during a busy shift. ### How do venue owners usually structure a placement deal with a machine operator?

Most placement deals start with a revenue split, commonly discussed at 50/50, though the exact figure is negotiated case by case and varies by market. Just as important as the percentage are who pays for repairs, who collects the cash, and how often the split gets paid out — settle those details before the machine arrives, not after the first busy month.

### Is arcade entertainment still relevant in bars given how much gaming has moved to phones and consoles?

Mobile and console gaming are largely solitary or online experiences, while a bartop arcade machine offers the opposite — a shared screen two people can stand around at the same table. That difference is exactly why the format has held on in bars for decades rather than being displaced by home gaming: it isn't competing with a phone, it's offering something a phone can't.
