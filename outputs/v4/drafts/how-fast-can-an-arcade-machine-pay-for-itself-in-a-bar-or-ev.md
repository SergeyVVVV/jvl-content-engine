# Arcade Machine for Bar ROI: How Fast Is Payback?

Ask how quickly an arcade machine pays for itself in a bar and you will get two kinds of answer. Catalog pages tell you the machine is a profit centre and show you a buy button. Forum threads tell you what somebody's cousin's tavern made one summer in 2019. Neither gives you a number you can check, and neither shows its working.

This article does the arithmetic in the open. Every revenue figure, every cost line, and every split percentage below is a stated assumption — chosen to be defensible rather than flattering — and the cost anchor is one real, published list price, so you can recompute any figure by hand and swap in your own numbers where these are wrong for your room.

The short version, so you know where this is going: on a base case of $75 a week in average coin and bill drop, a $4,250 commercial bartop machine that you own outright pays for itself in **just under 14 months** and then nets roughly $304 a month. A weak placement stretches that to about three years. A strong one closes it in about six and a half months. The gap between those outcomes is almost entirely foot traffic, and the second biggest variable is not the machine at all — it is whether you own it or someone else does.

One clarification before the numbers, because it is the point most competing content blurs. Venue revenue math only applies to a commercial-specification machine, meaning one fitted with a bill acceptor and a coin mechanism. A free-play home unit collects nothing, by design. If your interest is a machine for a basement or a home bar, the payback framing below does not apply to you at all, and that is stated again at the end.

## Why Arcade Machine ROI Questions Matter for Bar and Venue Owners

A commercial bartop machine is a four-figure purchase that competes for capital against things a bar already knows how to value. A new glasswasher has a repair bill it eliminates. An extra tap line has a margin per pour you can calculate from last month's invoices. An arcade machine has neither, which is exactly why the question gets asked so often and answered so badly: nobody buying one has a comparable line in their books to check it against.

That uncertainty has a cost of its own. Owners either avoid the purchase entirely, or they buy on a hunch and discover eighteen months later that the machine earns real but unremarkable money — money they would have been perfectly happy with had anyone told them the honest figure up front. The disappointment usually comes from the gap between gross coin drop, which is the number sellers quote, and net profit after the venue's share, service costs, and the slow months are taken out. Those are not the same number and they are frequently not even the same order of magnitude.

Event venues face a sharper version of the same problem. A bar has a steady weekly rhythm; a hall that books eleven Saturdays a quarter has clustered traffic and long dark stretches, and a machine sized on a wedding-reception night will look terrible on an annual average. Worse, plenty of event venues run their amusements on free play as part of a package, in which case the machine is an amenity rather than a revenue line and coin-drop math tells you nothing useful about whether it was a good buy.

So the useful question is not "is an arcade machine profitable" — with enough traffic almost any amusement device is. The useful question is how long your specific room takes to return $4,250, what it nets after that, and how confident you can reasonably be in that estimate. That is what the rest of this piece models.

## How Arcade Machine Revenue Actually Works: Revenue Split, Payment Model, and Venue Type

![Comparison table of three arcade machine payback scenarios: pessimistic, neutral, and optimistic, showing revenue, payback period, and monthly profit](https://zvsboppttbftxdsbcxym.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/article-images/how-fast-can-an-arcade-machine-pay-for-itself-in-a-bar-or-ev/inline-01.png)
*The three payback scenarios at a glance. The neutral case assumes $75 per week in average revenue; payback period varies from 6.5 months (optimistic) to 36 months (pessimistic).*

Revenue arrives one play at a time, through a coin mechanism, a bill acceptor, or both. There is no subscription, no card reader in the standard configuration, and no back-end reporting to reconcile — you open the machine, you count what is in the box. That simplicity is genuinely useful for modeling, because unlike food or drink there is no cost of goods to subtract. A dollar in the box is a dollar of gross margin before overhead.

What complicates it is ownership. Two arrangements dominate, and they produce completely different answers to "how fast is payback" because they start from completely different capital outlays.

| Arrangement | Who pays for the machine | Assumed split of gross drop | Who carries service costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Venue-owned | The venue, in full | Venue keeps 100% | Venue |
| Operator placement | An amusement operator | Commonly split around 50/50 | Operator |

Treat that 50/50 figure as an assumption for modeling, not a published standard — placement terms are negotiated locally and vary with how much traffic the venue brings. The structural point holds regardless of the exact percentage: if you buy the machine you keep everything and wait for payback, and if an operator places it you invest nothing and there is no payback period to calculate, only a revenue share that starts in week one. For a venue with tight capital, the operator route is often the better financial decision even though it earns less in absolute terms. That is worth saying plainly, because an article anchored to a purchase price has an obvious incentive not to say it.

Venue type then sets the ceiling. A neighbourhood bar with a steady weeknight crowd and people waiting on tables produces the most reliable drop, because arcade revenue is really a tax on idle time. A high-turnover cocktail room where nobody sits for ninety minutes produces less. An event space produces a great deal on booked nights and nothing at all in between, which is why the models below all work from an **average across the full year** rather than a good week.

Everything that follows assumes the venue-owned case, because that is the one with an actual payback period to compute. The operator variant appears wherever the difference matters.

## The Three Payback Scenarios Explained — and How Likely Each One Really Is

Single-number ROI claims are the tell that a page is selling something. Real placements vary enormously, so the honest format is a range with named assumptions attached to each end of it. Below are three scenarios: a weak placement, a base case, and a strong one. They are not equally likely, and treating them as though they were is the second most common failure in this category after inventing the numbers in the first place.

Every figure in this table is a stated assumption made for modeling purposes. None of it is sourced industry data, and it is better to say so plainly than to dress an estimate up as a statistic.

| Assumption | Pessimistic | Neutral (base case) | Optimistic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average weekly gross drop | $30 | $75 | $160 |
| Machine cost (list price) | $4,250 | $4,250 | $4,250 |
| Service / repair reserve | 5% of gross | 5% of gross | 5% of gross |
| Electricity | $5 / month | $5 / month | $5 / month |
| Venue share paid out | 0% (venue-owned) | 0% (venue-owned) | 0% (venue-owned) |
| Rough likelihood (editorial estimate) | ~25% | ~55% | ~20% |

The weekly figures are annual averages, not good weeks. This matters more than any other modeling choice in the article. If you take a strong Friday, multiply by fifty-two, and call it revenue, you will overstate the year badly, because December, February, and every stretch of bad weather are already inside the $75 base case.

The likelihood column is reasoned estimation rather than measured probability — there is no dataset behind it, and pretending otherwise would undercut every other figure here. It reflects a simple structural view: most placements land in the middle because most bars are ordinary bars, genuinely dead placements usually get moved or removed within a year, and the strong outcomes depend on conditions the buyer cannot manufacture.

One useful consequence of weighting them. Multiply each scenario's net monthly profit by its likelihood and the expected value comes to about $327 a month, slightly *above* the base case, because the upside tail is wider than the downside. That is a real feature of the economics — the worst case is bounded at roughly break-even, the best case is not. Plan around the base case anyway. You cannot pay an invoice with an expected value.

### Scenario 1 — Pessimistic Case: Assumptions and Payback Period

The weak placement assumes an average of **$30 a week** in gross drop across the whole year. In practice this is a machine in a quiet room, or a well-trafficked room where the machine is in the wrong corner — behind a pillar, past the restrooms, somewhere nobody stands while waiting for a drink. It is a real outcome and worth modeling, because the point of a downside case is to tell you what happens if you are wrong.

The arithmetic, step by step:

1. $30 per week × 52 weeks = **$1,560 gross per year**
2. $1,560 ÷ 12 = **$130.00 gross per month**
3. Service reserve at 5% of gross = $6.50 per month
4. Electricity at the assumed $5 per month
5. Net monthly profit = $130.00 − $6.50 − $5.00 = **$118.50**
6. Payback = $4,250 ÷ $118.50 = **35.9 months**

Call it thirty-six months. Three years to return the purchase price, after which the machine nets a little over $1,400 a year. That is not a disaster and it is not nothing — the machine does eventually pay for itself and then keeps paying — but three years is long enough that other things intervene. Rooms get refurbished. Leases end. The capital you tied up could have done something else.

What makes this scenario instructive is how it responds to intervention. Because the cost lines are small and largely fixed, almost the entire difference between this case and the base case is drop, and drop responds to placement more than to anything else. If you find yourself in this scenario six months in, moving the machine to a sightline near the bar rail is a free experiment with a much better expected return than any change to the machine itself. Give that experiment two months before concluding the room simply does not support an amusement device.

### Scenario 2 — Neutral / Most-Likely Case: Assumptions and Payback Period (What to Plan Around)

This is the number to build your decision on. The base case assumes an average of **$75 a week** in gross drop across the year — a machine in a normal bar with a normal weekly rhythm, visible from where people stand, with slow months already averaged in. If you take one figure away from this article, take this one.

The same six steps:

1. $75 per week × 52 weeks = **$3,900 gross per year**
2. $3,900 ÷ 12 = **$325.00 gross per month**
3. Service reserve at 5% of gross = $16.25 per month
4. Electricity at the assumed $5 per month
5. Net monthly profit = $325.00 − $16.25 − $5.00 = **$303.75**
6. Payback = $4,250 ÷ $303.75 = **14.0 months**

Fourteen months. Slightly over a year to recover a $4,250 purchase, and from month fifteen onward the machine contributes roughly $304 a month to the bottom line with no cost of goods behind it. On these assumptions that is a strong rate of return on capital — though the comparison to other bar equipment is a judgement, not a measured one. It is also, in absolute terms, modest. Three hundred dollars a month will not rescue a struggling room. It is roughly a decent Saturday's bar margin, arriving quietly every month without staffing.

Now the ownership variant, because it reframes the whole calculation. If an operator owns the machine and you split the same $325 gross fifty-fifty, the operator receives $162.50, carries the $16.25 service reserve, and nets $146.25 a month — a payback of about **29 months** on their $4,250. The venue in that arrangement receives $162.50 a month having spent nothing at all and having no payback period to worry about.

So the honest reading of the base case is this: buying the machine roughly doubles your monthly return and costs you fourteen months of capital to get there. If your room's traffic is uncertain, letting someone else carry that risk for half the drop is a perfectly rational choice, not a lesser one.

### Scenario 3 — Optimistic Case: Assumptions and Payback Period

The strong placement assumes an average of **$160 a week**, sustained across the year. Think of a busy room with real waiting time — a sports bar during season, a taproom with long tables and no table service, a venue where a machine sits between the bar and the only door. The conditions that produce this are mostly structural, which is why this model weights it at roughly one placement in five rather than treating it as an achievable target.

The arithmetic runs the same way:

1. $160 per week × 52 weeks = **$8,320 gross per year**
2. $8,320 ÷ 12 = **$693.33 gross per month**
3. Service reserve at 5% of gross = $34.67 per month
4. Electricity at the assumed $5 per month
5. Net monthly profit = $693.33 − $34.67 − $5.00 = **$653.66**
6. Payback = $4,250 ÷ $653.66 = **6.5 months**

Six and a half months, and thereafter close to $7,850 a year in net contribution. Those are the figures that make arcade machines sound like a licence to print money, and they are also the figures to be most wary of as a planning basis. Not because they are impossible — they clearly are not — but because the traffic that produces them is a property of the room, not of the purchase, and you generally know whether you have that room before you buy anything.

There is a practical test. If you already run another coin-operated amusement — a pool table, a jukebox, a claw machine — you have a real revenue history for exactly the behaviour this model depends on. Take that machine's actual annual drop, adjust for the difference in price per play, and you have a far better estimate for your room than the $160 assumed here or any figure from a sales page. A venue with a pool table clearing $200 a week has strong evidence for the optimistic case. A venue whose jukebox averages $25 should be modeling the pessimistic one, whatever the catalog says.

## What the Machine Earns After It Pays for Itself: Net Monthly Profit by Scenario

Payback is only half the question, and it is the less interesting half. A machine that returns its cost in fourteen months and then dies in month sixteen is a bad purchase; one that takes three years and runs for ten is a good one. What matters after break-even is the annual contribution and how long the hardware keeps producing it.

Here is the post-payback picture, carried directly from the weekly assumptions above with no new figures introduced.

| Scenario | Net monthly profit | Net annual profit | Annual return on $4,250 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pessimistic | $118.50 | $1,422 | ~33% |
| Neutral (base case) | $303.75 | $3,645 | ~86% |
| Optimistic | $653.66 | $7,844 | ~185% |

Those return percentages are the part that surprises people, and they deserve a caveat immediately. An 86% annual return on capital sounds extraordinary compared with anything in a financial market, but it is not comparable to one. It is a return on a depreciating physical asset that requires floor space, occasional attention, and a functioning venue around it, and it is only available up to the ceiling of one machine's drop. You cannot deploy $425,000 at 86% by buying a hundred of them; the tenth machine in a room earns close to nothing.

The useful way to read the table is over the life of the hardware. In the base case, sixty months of ownership produces $18,225 in net profit against the $4,250 outlay — **$13,975 clear** across five years. The weak placement produces $7,110 gross of net profit over the same period, or $2,860 clear, which is a real but thin result. The strong placement produces close to $35,000 clear, which is why operators keep placing machines even though most placements are ordinary.

One thing this table does not capture, and this model deliberately leaves it out because no defensible number is available for it: an amusement device that holds a group in the room for an extra thirty minutes sells additional drinks, and that indirect revenue may exceed the coin drop entirely. If you believe that effect is real in your venue, it improves every scenario above. It just cannot be measured from the cash box.

## The Recurring Costs That Quietly Erode Arcade Profit

The 5% service reserve and the $5 electricity line in this model are small, and that is not an accident of the assumptions — the ongoing costs of a bartop machine genuinely are low compared with almost anything else a bar plugs in. But small is not zero, and the four cost categories below behave differently enough that lumping them together will mislead you.

> **[VISUAL]** *diagram — a simple waterfall showing $325 gross monthly drop reducing through revenue share, service reserve, and electricity to $303.75 net, in the base case*

**Service and repair** is the line most people underestimate, and its size depends heavily on what the machine's primary interface is. A joystick-and-button machine concentrates wear in exactly the components customers grip, hammer, and spill into; microswitches and stick shafts are consumables on a busy placement. A machine controlled primarily through a touchscreen removes that particular category of wear part from the equation. It does not remove failure — screens, power supplies, and audio hardware all fail eventually, and any claim to the contrary should make you suspicious of everything else on the page — but it meaningfully reduces exposure to the small, frequent repairs that make up most of an amusement machine's service history. The 5% reserve here assumes the touchscreen case. Budget higher for mechanical controls.

**Electricity** is the least solid line in the model. It uses $5 a month as a placeholder rather than a figure derived from any particular machine's rated draw, because the correct number depends on wattage, your operating hours, and your commercial rate per kilowatt-hour. Take the wattage from the specification sheet of whatever unit you are considering, multiply it out, and replace the placeholder. It will not change the payback period by much — even doubling it moves the base case by less than a week — but you should know it is a placeholder.

**Revenue share** is the largest cost in the model whenever it applies, and it applies whenever you do not own the machine. Half of gross is not a fee; it is the single biggest determinant of your payback period after traffic itself, as the twenty-nine-month operator figure earlier showed.

**Seasonality** is not a cost line at all, which is precisely why it catches people. It does not appear on any invoice. It shows up as a July that funds three months of the year and a February that funds none, and the only defence against it is doing what this article has done throughout: model on an annual average and never on a good week.

## How to Plan Realistically Before Buying an Arcade Machine for a Bar or Venue

The model in this article is a framework, not a forecast for your room. Its value is that every input is visible and replaceable, so the sensible next step is to replace the estimated inputs with figures you can actually observe. Here is a sensible order to work through them.

1. **Find a real drop figure from your own venue.** Any existing coin-operated device — pool table, jukebox, dartboard — gives you a measured baseline for how your customers behave with an amusement machine. Use it in place of the weekly assumption used here.
2. **Decide the ownership question before the machine question.** If capital is tight or traffic is unproven, an operator placement at roughly half the drop carries no payback risk at all. Buying only wins if you are reasonably confident of the base case.
3. **Set the weekly figure as an annual average.** Take your best guess, then subtract for the two or three slowest months. If the number still supports the purchase, it is a real number.
4. **Confirm the current list price and the electricity draw in writing.** Both inputs above are flagged in this article as needing verification; get them from the manufacturer's current specification rather than from an article.
5. **Choose the placement before you order.** A sightline from the bar rail is worth more to your payback period than any feature on the spec sheet.

On the downside case specifically, warranty terms deserve a line in the model even though they rarely appear in one. The ECHO carries a 1-year all-inclusive manufacturer warranty with JVL covering shipping in both directions for warranty service, which matters more for a 39-pound machine than it sounds — return freight on a failed unit is the kind of unbudgeted cost that turns a thin pessimistic case into a loss. Combined with the individual pre-shipment testing described above, it puts a floor under the first year of ownership.

If you are running venue numbers, the practical next step is to review the current [Commercial Edition specifications and list pricing on the ECHO product page](https://jvl.ca/en/echo) and rebuild the three scenarios above with your own weekly figure in place of the ones used here.

And if you have read this far because the machine is for your own basement, home bar, or den rather than a business, none of the payback math applies to you — the residential unit runs on free play and has no cash handling at all. The [ECHO for home overview](https://jvl.ca/en/home) is the relevant page, and the question there is simply whether you will use it, not when it pays for itself.

## What the Numbers Actually Tell You

The headline answer is fourteen months. In the base case — $75 a week in average drop, a machine you own outright, a small service reserve and a modest power bill — a $4,250 commercial bartop machine returns its purchase price in just under fourteen months and then contributes about $304 a month, or $3,645 a year, indefinitely. Plan around that figure. Treat six and a half months as a pleasant surprise and three years as the outcome you can survive.

What should stay with you is not the number itself but which variables moved it. Foot traffic accounts for essentially the entire spread between the three scenarios, because the cost lines are small and largely fixed. Ownership structure comes second and is the only variable you control completely: buying doubles your return and costs you a year of tied-up capital, while an operator placement earns less and risks nothing. Everything else — electricity, service, even a doubling of the maintenance reserve — moves the payback date by days, not months. Machines do not fail as investments because the repair budget was wrong. They fail because the room was quiet.

Be honest, too, about what a good result looks like here. Three hundred dollars a month is a strong return on capital and a small line in a bar's accounts. It is worth having, it is genuinely low-maintenance income, and it will not change the trajectory of a venue by itself. Anyone telling you otherwise is selling you the optimistic scenario at base-case odds.

So run your own version. Take your real drop history, your real electricity rate, and the current published price of whatever machine you are considering, and rebuild the six lines of arithmetic in this article. It takes about ten minutes, and it is the only version of this calculation that is actually about your venue.

## FAQ

### Are arcade machines still profitable now that most people have smartphones?

Yes, in venues with genuine dwell time — the machine competes for idle attention while people wait for a drink or a table, not for screen time on a phone already in someone's pocket. Bars, taprooms, and rec venues with steady foot traffic still generate a consistent average weekly drop, though the effect is narrower than it was decades ago. Venues without natural waiting time — fast turnover, no standing room, no queue — don't have this dynamic and shouldn't plan around it.

### How many arcade machines does a typical bar need?

Most bars start with one and only add a second if the first clearly earns its keep, because additional machines compete with each other for the same pool of idle customers rather than adding proportional new drop. A large venue with genuinely separate waiting zones — say, near the bar and near a pool table — can sometimes support two, but doubling machine count rarely doubles revenue. Track the first unit's drop for several months before considering another.

### What's the difference between a bartop arcade machine and a full-size cabinet for bar revenue?

A bartop unit sits on an existing counter or a small stand, so it adds a revenue source without claiming dedicated floor space — a real advantage where square footage is already committed to seating. A full-size cabinet needs its own footprint and, because it typically costs more upfront, needs a higher weekly drop to match the same payback period. For most bar and small-venue placements, the smaller footprint of a bartop machine gives it a payback edge independent of the games themselves.

### Can a bar negotiate the revenue split with an arcade machine operator?

Yes — the roughly 50/50 split used for modelling in this article is a common starting point, not a fixed industry rate, and operators typically adjust it based on the venue's foot traffic, exclusivity, and floor space offered. A venue with strong, provable traffic has real negotiating leverage; a new or unproven room has less. Get the agreed split, contract length, and who carries service costs written down before the machine is installed.
